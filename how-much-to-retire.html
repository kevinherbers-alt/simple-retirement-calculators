How Much Do You Actually Need to Retire?

The real answer — and why it's different for everyone.

There's no universal retirement number. The figure depends on what you plan to spend, what income you'll have from other sources, how long you'll live, and what kind of flexibility you're willing to build in. But the framework for calculating it is the same for everyone, and it's straightforward once you understand the pieces.

Start with your spending, not your salary

The most common mistake in retirement planning is targeting a number based on pre-retirement income — the "replace 80% of your salary" rule of thumb. That approach ignores the actual question: how much will you spend in retirement?

For some people, retirement spending is significantly lower than working-life spending. Commuting costs go away. Work clothes go away. Maybe the mortgage is paid off. Kids are grown. For others — especially those planning to travel extensively or maintain active lifestyles — spending stays the same or increases in the early years of retirement.

Start by estimating your annual retirement spending as concretely as possible: housing, food, healthcare, travel, leisure, insurance. That number is your foundation for everything else.

The core calculation

Once you know your annual spending, subtract any guaranteed income you'll have — Social Security, a pension, rental income. The remaining gap is what your investment portfolio needs to cover each year.

Divide that annual gap by your withdrawal rate (4% is the standard starting point, or 0.04) to get your portfolio target. Alternatively, multiply it by 25 — same math, different framing.

Example: You expect to spend $70,000 a year. Social Security will cover $25,000. Your portfolio needs to produce $45,000 annually. At a 4% withdrawal rate, your target is $45,000 / 0.04 = $1,125,000.

The multiplier changes with your withdrawal rate. At 3.5%, multiply by ~28.6. At 3%, multiply by ~33. A more conservative rate means a larger target — but also a portfolio that's significantly less likely to run dry in a long retirement.

Healthcare is the wild card

For retirees under 65 who aren't yet Medicare-eligible, healthcare costs can be one of the largest and most unpredictable budget items. Private insurance premiums for a 60-year-old couple can run $1,500-2,500/month before the ACA marketplace. Even after Medicare eligibility, out-of-pocket costs, supplemental insurance, and long-term care add up significantly.

Many retirement planning models underestimate healthcare. If you're planning to retire before 65, add a specific healthcare line item to your spending estimate rather than hoping it averages out.

Social Security timing matters more than most people realize

You can claim Social Security as early as 62 or as late as 70. Each year you delay past your full retirement age (66-67 for most people currently) increases your benefit by approximately 8%. Delaying from 62 to 70 can increase your monthly benefit by 75% or more.

The break-even analysis is straightforward: if you delay claiming, you need to live long enough for the higher monthly payments to exceed what you would have collected by claiming early. For most people, the break-even is around age 78-80. If you expect to live past 80, delaying generally pays off.

Crucially: delaying Social Security reduces how much your portfolio needs to cover annually, which can significantly reduce your retirement number. A couple that delays claiming until 70 might need $300,000-500,000 less in savings than one that claims at 62.

Inflation erodes purchasing power

A retirement that lasts 30 years involves significant inflation exposure. At 3% annual inflation, $70,000 in today's dollars requires $113,000 to buy the same things in 20 years. Your retirement income — whether from a portfolio or fixed sources — needs to keep pace.

Social Security is inflation-adjusted (COLA increases each year). Most pensions are not. Portfolio withdrawals can be adjusted, but that adjustment draws down capital faster.

Common questions

Is $1 million enough to retire?

At a 4% withdrawal rate, $1 million produces $40,000 per year from your portfolio. Add Social Security and that may be plenty — or it may fall short, depending on your spending and where you live. In a high cost-of-living area with significant spending, $1 million may not be enough. In a lower cost area with Social Security covering a meaningful portion of expenses, it often is. Run your specific numbers rather than assuming a round figure works.

What if I retire early before Social Security kicks in?

Early retirees face a two-phase problem: funding the years before Social Security starts (potentially 10-15 years) entirely from savings, then transitioning to a lower portfolio withdrawal rate once guaranteed income arrives. This usually means needing a larger initial portfolio. Some early retirees use a "bridge strategy" — drawing down savings more heavily in the early years while delaying Social Security to maximize the eventual benefit.

How do I account for a spouse with different retirement timing?

If spouses retire at different times, the household income picture changes at each transition point. Model each phase separately: what does the household spend and earn in year 1, year 5, year 10? Include both Social Security benefits (both spouses can claim) and any difference in portfolio drawdown needs at each stage.

Should I include home equity in my retirement number?

It depends on whether you plan to access it. If you intend to downsize and invest the proceeds, yes — that's a real asset. If you plan to stay in your home indefinitely, it's harder to use without a reverse mortgage or home equity line. Most retirement planning treats home equity as a backstop rather than a primary income source.
