What Is the 4% Rule? The Research Behind Retirement's Most Famous Guideline

The rule that launched a thousand retirement calculators — and what the original research actually said.

The 4% rule is the most cited guideline in retirement planning, and also one of the most misunderstood. It gets treated as a magic number, a guarantee, or sometimes a relic that no longer applies. None of those framings are quite right. Here's what the research actually found and how to use it honestly.

Where it came from: the Trinity Study

The 4% rule originates from a 1998 paper by three professors at Trinity University — Philip Cooley, Carl Hubbard, and Daniel Walz. They analyzed historical stock and bond returns from 1926 through 1995 to answer a specific question: what withdrawal rate would have allowed a retiree to sustain their portfolio for various time periods without running out of money?

Their finding: a portfolio of roughly 50-75% stocks, withdrawing 4% of the initial balance annually (adjusted each year for inflation), had a very high success rate over 30-year retirement periods in the historical data. That's the source of the rule.

Importantly, the original study defined "success" as not running out of money — not necessarily leaving an inheritance or preserving the full portfolio value. The 4% rate was the highest rate that maintained a high (but not 100%) success rate over 30 years in historical data.

What the rule actually says — and doesn't say

The 4% rule says: if you withdraw 4% of your starting portfolio in year one, and adjust that dollar amount for inflation each subsequent year, historical data suggests your portfolio would have survived 30 years in most historical scenarios.

It does not say:

  • Your portfolio is guaranteed to last 30 years
  • 4% is the right rate for retirements longer than 30 years
  • It works regardless of what the market does at the start of your retirement
  • It applies equally to all portfolio compositions
The 4% rule is a starting point for planning, not a guarantee of outcomes. It's based on what worked historically — which is valuable context, but not a promise about the future.

The sequence of returns risk

One of the biggest risks the rule doesn't fully protect against is sequence of returns risk — the danger that a significant market downturn early in retirement can permanently damage a portfolio, even if long-term average returns are fine.

Consider two retirees who both experience a 7% average annual return over 30 years. The one who gets the bad years first (say, a crash in year 2 and year 3) may run out of money even though their average return matches the one who got good years early and bad years late. When you're withdrawing from a portfolio, losing money early is disproportionately damaging because you have less capital remaining to benefit from the eventual recovery.

This is why many financial planners suggest a slightly lower rate — 3.5% or 3% — for people retiring in their 50s or early 60s who face longer potential retirement periods, or for anyone concerned about retiring into a period of high market valuations.

How inflation affects the rule

The rule assumes you increase your withdrawal by the inflation rate each year. If you withdraw $40,000 in year one and inflation is 3%, you withdraw $41,200 in year two, $42,436 in year three, and so on. This is what the original research tested.

In periods of high inflation — like the early 1980s or the 2021-2023 period — this adjustment puts significant additional strain on the portfolio. The rule held up historically even through high-inflation periods, but it's worth recognizing that inflation is one of the larger variables in long-term retirement sustainability.

Adapting the rule to your situation

The 4% rule is best used as an anchor for planning, not a rigid formula. Some ways people adapt it:

  • Lower rate for longer retirements: Retiring at 55 with a potential 40-year retirement? 3.5% or even 3% builds in more cushion.
  • Higher rate with flexibility: If you're willing to reduce spending in bad market years, you may be able to sustain a slightly higher rate over time.
  • Social Security as a floor: Once Social Security kicks in, you need less from your portfolio, which effectively reduces your withdrawal rate from savings.
  • Bucket strategy: Some retirees keep 1-2 years of expenses in cash, insulating themselves from being forced to sell equities in a down market.

Common questions

Is the 4% rule still valid today?

It depends on who you ask. Some researchers argue that with current market valuations and lower expected bond returns, a safer rate may be closer to 3-3.5%. Others point out that the original research survived periods of high valuations before and that the rule remains a reasonable baseline. The honest answer: it's a reasonable planning guideline, not a certainty, and a slightly lower rate adds meaningful safety margin.

What portfolio mix does the rule assume?

The original Trinity Study found the 4% rate worked best with a 50-75% stock allocation. A 100% bond portfolio at 4% historically struggled. Most financial advisors suggest a retirement portfolio of at least 50% equities for someone using a 4% withdrawal rate.

What if I have a pension or Social Security?

Those income sources reduce what your portfolio needs to cover. If your expenses are $60,000/year and Social Security pays $20,000, your portfolio only needs to produce $40,000 — so your retirement number is $40,000 divided by your withdrawal rate, not $60,000. The retirement number calculator accounts for this directly.

Does the 4% rule account for taxes?

No — it's a pre-tax calculation. If your withdrawals are from a traditional 401(k) or IRA, you'll owe income tax on them, which means you need to withdraw more than your actual spending target. The after-tax impact depends heavily on your tax bracket, state taxes, and account mix. A financial planner can model the tax implications for your specific situation.

How do I calculate my retirement number using the 4% rule?

Multiply your annual portfolio income need (total spending minus any guaranteed income) by 25. Need $40,000 from your portfolio? Your number is $1,000,000. Need $60,000? It's $1,500,000. The calculator does this automatically and shows how the number changes at different withdrawal rates.
