Roth vs. Traditional 401(k) and IRA: Which Is Better for Retirement?

The tax question that shapes your retirement income — and how to think through it honestly.

The Roth vs. traditional debate is one of the most common retirement planning questions, and the answer is genuinely different for different people. The core trade-off is simple: pay taxes now (Roth) or pay taxes later (traditional). But the right choice depends on factors that are hard to predict — your future tax rate, how long until retirement, and what the tax code looks like decades from now.

How each account works

Traditional 401(k) and IRA: Contributions are made with pre-tax money (reducing your taxable income today). The money grows tax-deferred. You pay ordinary income tax on withdrawals in retirement.

Roth 401(k) and Roth IRA: Contributions are made with after-tax money (no deduction today). The money grows tax-free. Qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free.

Both accounts benefit from tax-advantaged compounding — the difference is when you pay the tax.

When traditional wins

Traditional accounts favor you when your current tax rate is higher than your expected retirement tax rate. This is the most common situation for high earners in their peak earning years. If you're in the 32% bracket now and expect to be in the 22% bracket in retirement, every dollar you contribute to a traditional account saves you 10 cents in taxes per dollar.

Traditional also makes sense if you're not sure you'll stay in a high tax bracket — the immediate deduction is a guaranteed benefit, while the future tax-free treatment is speculative.

When Roth wins

Roth accounts favor you when your current tax rate is lower than your expected future rate. This is particularly relevant for:

  • Early-career workers in lower tax brackets who expect income to grow significantly
  • Anyone who expects tax rates in general to rise over their career (uncertain but plausible)
  • High earners who want tax diversification in retirement — having both taxable and tax-free income sources
  • People who don't need the money and want to pass it to heirs (Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions during the owner's lifetime)
Roth accounts also have a practical advantage: contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time without penalty. This makes a Roth IRA a flexible emergency fund backup for people early in their careers, in addition to its retirement function.

The case for both

Many financial advisors recommend contributing to both types — tax diversification in retirement. Having a mix of traditional and Roth assets gives you flexibility to manage your tax bracket in retirement by choosing which account to draw from each year.

In a year when you have large deductible expenses, you might draw from the traditional account. In a year when you're in a lower bracket, you might draw from Roth. This flexibility is genuinely valuable and can reduce your lifetime tax bill significantly.

Income limits and rules to know

Roth IRA contributions are subject to income limits. In 2024, the ability to contribute directly phases out at $146,000-$161,000 for single filers and $230,000-$240,000 for married filers. Above those limits, higher earners often use the "backdoor Roth" — contributing to a traditional IRA and then converting it — which has no income limit.

Traditional IRA deductibility also phases out at certain incomes if you or your spouse have access to a workplace retirement plan. Check current IRS limits, as these adjust annually.

Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) start at age 73 for traditional accounts, forcing withdrawals whether you need the money or not. Roth IRAs have no RMDs during your lifetime, which is a significant advantage for estate planning.

Common questions

If I can only choose one, which should it be?

Early in your career, Roth generally makes more sense — your tax rate is likely near its lifetime low, and decades of tax-free growth are enormously valuable. Mid-to-late career with peak earnings, traditional often makes more sense for the immediate deduction. If in doubt and you have access to both, split contributions between the two.

Can I convert traditional savings to Roth?

Yes — a Roth conversion moves money from a traditional account to a Roth, and you pay income tax on the converted amount in the year of conversion. This makes most sense in years when your income is temporarily lower (early retirement, a career gap, a low-income year) and you can convert at a lower tax rate than you'd otherwise face on withdrawals.

Does my employer match go into the same account?

Employer matches often go into traditional accounts even if you contribute to a Roth 401(k) — check your plan documents. The match is still valuable; it just comes with a tax bill on withdrawal. Some plans now allow Roth matching, but it's not universal.

What about the Roth 401(k) vs. Roth IRA?

Both offer tax-free growth and withdrawals. The 401(k) has higher contribution limits ($23,000 in 2024 vs. $7,000 for an IRA) and no income limits for contributions. The IRA offers more investment flexibility — you're not limited to your employer's fund options. If you can afford to max both, do. If not, many advisors suggest maxing the 401(k) match first, then the Roth IRA, then additional 401(k).
