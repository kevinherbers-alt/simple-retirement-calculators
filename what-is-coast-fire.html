What Is Coast FIRE? How to Know If Your Savings Can Grow Without You

The FIRE milestone that changes how you think about saving — and why it matters even if early retirement isn't your goal.

Coast FIRE is the point at which you've saved enough that, even if you stop contributing entirely, your existing savings will grow to your retirement number by the time you want to retire — purely through investment returns, with no additional contributions.

It's called "Coast" because once you hit it, you can coast the rest of the way. Your money does the heavy lifting from that point on.

How Coast FIRE is calculated

The math works backward from your retirement target. Start with your retirement number (what you need at retirement). Then discount it back to today using an expected return rate and the number of years until retirement. The result is your Coast FIRE number — the amount you need saved today to reach your target without another contribution.

Formula: Coast FIRE Number = Retirement Target / (1 + annual return rate) ^ years until retirement

Example: You want $1,000,000 at retirement in 25 years, and you expect 7% average annual returns. Your Coast FIRE number is $1,000,000 / (1.07)^25 = approximately $184,249. If you have $184,249 saved today, you're Coast FIRE — your savings will reach $1,000,000 in 25 years without another dollar contributed.

Coast FIRE doesn't mean you stop saving — it means additional savings beyond your basic expenses are now optional rather than necessary. Many people hit Coast FIRE and keep contributing anyway, which accelerates their timeline to full financial independence.

Why Coast FIRE matters even if you don't want to retire early

Most people think of FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) as an all-or-nothing goal. Coast FIRE is more nuanced — it's a milestone that changes what's at stake in your career decisions, regardless of whether early retirement is your plan.

Once you're Coast FIRE, you can:

  • Take a lower-paying job you find more meaningful without derailing retirement
  • Work part-time or freelance to cover living expenses, without needing to save aggressively
  • Take an extended sabbatical or career break
  • Start a business with lower risk, since retirement is already funded
  • Reduce work hours to prioritize family, health, or other priorities

The flexibility Coast FIRE creates doesn't require actually stopping work — it just changes the calculus of every career and financial decision you make from that point forward.

The role of return rate assumptions

Coast FIRE calculations are highly sensitive to your assumed return rate. At 7%, you need to save considerably more than at 9% — because each dollar has less time to compound to the same target. At 5%, the required savings are dramatically higher.

Most financial planners use 6-7% as a conservative long-term real (inflation-adjusted) return assumption for a diversified stock/bond portfolio. Using a lower rate builds in a margin of safety. Using a higher rate produces a more optimistic picture but increases the risk that your savings fall short if markets underperform.

Coast FIRE vs. other FIRE variants

Lean FIRE: Retiring early on a very frugal budget — typically $25,000-40,000/year for a couple. Requires a smaller portfolio but leaves less margin for unexpected expenses.

Fat FIRE: Retiring early with a comfortable or generous spending level — typically $100,000+/year. Requires a much larger portfolio but provides more cushion.

Barista FIRE: Having enough saved to supplement income from part-time work. Similar to Coast FIRE in spirit — you're not fully retired but don't need aggressive career earnings to be secure.

Full FIRE: Having enough to cover all expenses without any work income, indefinitely.

Coast FIRE is usually the first major milestone on any of these paths — the point where the portfolio becomes self-sustaining given enough time.

Common questions

What return rate should I use for Coast FIRE calculations?

6-7% is a common conservative assumption for a diversified stock-heavy portfolio over long periods, in real (inflation-adjusted) terms. Some people use nominal returns (before inflation adjustment) of 8-10%, which produces a more optimistic number but doesn't account for inflation eroding purchasing power. For planning purposes, inflation-adjusted returns give you a more honest picture.

Does Coast FIRE account for Social Security?

It depends on how you define your retirement target. If your retirement number already accounts for Social Security as a supplement (reducing how much your portfolio needs to produce), then yes — it's baked in. If your retirement number assumes zero Social Security, you're being conservative. Many Coast FIRE calculations ignore Social Security entirely as a margin of safety.

What if I've already passed my Coast FIRE number?

Congratulations — your retirement savings are effectively on autopilot. You still need income to cover current expenses, but every additional dollar you save accelerates your timeline rather than being essential to it. Many people in this position shift toward work they find more meaningful rather than maximizing income.

Is Coast FIRE realistic for average earners?

For someone who starts saving consistently in their 20s, Coast FIRE by their late 30s or early 40s is achievable even on moderate incomes. Time is the most powerful variable — someone who saves aggressively for 10 years starting at 25 may hit Coast FIRE before someone who earns more but starts at 35. The earlier the start, the more compounding does the work.
