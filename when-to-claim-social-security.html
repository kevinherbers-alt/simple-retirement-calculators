When Should You Claim Social Security? How Timing Affects Your Benefit

One of the biggest financial decisions of retirement — and why the math is more nuanced than "wait as long as possible."

When to claim Social Security is one of the most consequential financial decisions most Americans make. Claim early and you get smaller payments for longer. Claim late and you get larger payments for fewer years. The right choice depends on your health, finances, and how long you expect to live — but a few core principles apply to almost everyone.

The basics: ages and benefit amounts

You can claim Social Security retirement benefits as early as age 62 or as late as age 70. The age at which you receive your "full" (unreduced) benefit is your Full Retirement Age (FRA), which is 66-67 depending on your birth year:

  • Born 1943-1954: FRA is 66
  • Born 1955-1959: FRA rises gradually from 66 to 67
  • Born 1960 or later: FRA is 67

Claiming before your FRA permanently reduces your benefit. Claiming after your FRA increases it by approximately 8% per year, up to age 70. After 70, there's no additional benefit to waiting.

The break-even analysis

If you delay claiming, you give up payments in the early years in exchange for higher payments later. The break-even point is the age at which total lifetime benefits from the delayed strategy exceed total benefits from the early strategy.

For claiming at 62 vs. 67 (a typical comparison), the break-even is around age 78-80 in most scenarios. If you live past 80, delaying generally pays off in total dollars. If you don't live to 80, claiming early was the better financial decision.

The break-even analysis looks at dollars, but it ignores the time value of money — money received early can be invested. Accounting for investment returns makes the break-even age somewhat higher, which weakens the case for delay. Neither framing gives a complete picture.

When claiming early makes sense

  • Health concerns: If you have reason to expect a shorter-than-average lifespan, claiming early often makes mathematical sense.
  • Financial need: If you need the income at 62 to cover essential expenses and the alternative is depleting savings or going into debt, claiming early is the pragmatic choice.
  • You'll invest the money: If you don't need the early payments for expenses, investing them may produce returns that change the break-even calculus.
  • Spousal benefit coordination: In some two-earner households, one spouse claiming early while the other delays can optimize total household benefits.

When delaying makes sense

  • Good health and family longevity: If you're healthy and your family tends to be long-lived, the bigger benefit from delaying has more years to pay out.
  • You're still working: Benefits before FRA are reduced if you earn above a threshold. If you're still working, delaying avoids this reduction.
  • You have other income: If a pension, portfolio withdrawals, or a spouse's income can cover expenses until 70, delaying to maximize your benefit makes sense.
  • Survivor benefit: For married couples, the higher earner's benefit becomes the survivor benefit when one spouse dies. Maximizing that benefit by delaying protects the surviving spouse.

The survivor benefit consideration

For married couples, the survivor benefit is one of the most important factors in the timing decision. When one spouse dies, the surviving spouse receives the higher of their own benefit or their deceased spouse's benefit — not both.

This means the higher earner delaying to 70 can significantly increase the lifetime income of a long-lived surviving spouse, even if the higher earner themselves doesn't live long enough to break even personally. For couples where there's a meaningful age or health difference between spouses, this calculation often tips decisively toward delaying.

Common questions

What if Social Security won't exist when I retire?

The Social Security trust fund has projected funding challenges, but the program is unlikely to disappear. Current projections suggest benefits may be reduced to approximately 75-80% of scheduled levels if no changes are made — but Congress has consistently acted to shore up the program before cuts take effect. Planning assuming zero Social Security is very conservative; planning assuming full scheduled benefits may be slightly optimistic. Many planners suggest assuming 75-85% of current projected benefits as a middle ground.

How does working while receiving Social Security affect my benefit?

Before your Full Retirement Age, earning above a threshold reduces your Social Security benefit temporarily — $1 in benefits is withheld for every $2 earned above the limit. After FRA, you can earn any amount without reduction. The withheld benefits aren't lost — they're added back as a higher monthly payment once you reach FRA.

Can I change my mind after claiming?

Within 12 months of first claiming, you can withdraw your application and repay all benefits received, essentially undoing the claim. After that window, your options are limited to suspending benefits (if you're between FRA and 70) to earn delayed credits going forward. This is worth knowing about if your financial situation changes shortly after you start claiming.

How do I find out what my benefit will be?

Create an account at ssa.gov to see your Social Security statement, which shows your projected benefit at 62, your FRA, and 70. The estimate is based on your actual earnings history and is the most accurate planning number available.
