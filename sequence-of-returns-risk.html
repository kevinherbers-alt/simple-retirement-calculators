Sequence of Returns Risk: Why Market Timing in Retirement Matters More Than You Think

The retirement risk that average return calculations completely miss — and how to protect against it.

You could have a perfectly average investment return over 30 years and still run out of money in retirement. This isn't a paradox — it's the result of sequence of returns risk, one of the most important and underappreciated concepts in retirement planning.

What sequence of returns risk is

Sequence of returns risk is the danger that the order of investment returns matters enormously when you're withdrawing from a portfolio — even when the long-term average return is identical.

Consider two retirees, both starting with $1,000,000 and withdrawing $50,000 per year (5%), both experiencing the same average annual return of 7% over 20 years. The only difference: Retiree A gets the bad years early (large losses in years 1-3), while Retiree B gets the bad years late (large losses in years 17-20). Same average return, same withdrawal amount — but Retiree A may deplete their portfolio years before Retiree B.

Why? Because when you withdraw from a declining portfolio, you're selling shares at low prices. Those sold shares can't participate in the eventual recovery. The math is permanently impaired in a way it isn't during the accumulation phase, when losses just mean you're buying shares cheaply.

During accumulation, a market crash is a buying opportunity. During retirement, a market crash forces you to sell at the worst possible time. The same event has opposite effects depending on which phase of your financial life you're in.

Why the first decade of retirement is the most vulnerable

The risk is most acute in the early years of retirement for a simple reason: your portfolio is at its largest, and therefore has the most to lose in dollar terms. A 30% decline in year 2 of retirement on a $1,000,000 portfolio is a $300,000 loss. The same 30% decline in year 20, on a portfolio that's been drawn down to $400,000, is a $120,000 loss. The early years are where the damage is hardest to recover from.

This is why financial planners focus so much attention on the first 5-10 years of retirement. Protecting the portfolio in that window significantly improves the odds of long-term sustainability, even if it means accepting somewhat lower returns.

Strategies to manage the risk

The bucket strategy: Keep 1-3 years of expenses in cash or very short-term bonds. During a market downturn, draw from the cash bucket rather than selling equities. This gives the stock portion time to recover before you need to touch it. The psychological benefit — knowing you don't have to sell in a crash — is as valuable as the mathematical one.

Flexible withdrawal rates: Instead of withdrawing a fixed inflation-adjusted dollar amount each year, adjust withdrawals based on portfolio performance. In good years, spend a little more. In bad years, cut discretionary expenses. Studies show this flexibility dramatically improves portfolio survival rates compared to rigid withdrawals.

Delaying Social Security: Delaying Social Security to 70 increases the guaranteed income floor, which reduces how much you need to withdraw from the portfolio in early retirement — the most vulnerable years. This is one of the most effective sequence-of-returns risk management strategies available.

Bond tent / rising equity glidepath: Some researchers recommend being more conservative (higher bond allocation) at the start of retirement and gradually increasing equity exposure as you age. This is counterintuitive — most allocation models say decrease equities as you age — but it specifically targets the sequence risk in early retirement.

Part-time work: Even modest earned income in the early years of retirement dramatically reduces portfolio dependence when the portfolio is largest and most vulnerable. A few years of part-time work early in retirement can meaningfully improve long-term portfolio survival.

What the drawdown calculator models

The drawdown calculator on this site models portfolio depletion under a fixed return rate assumption — which doesn't capture sequence risk. To get a sense of the risk, try running the calculator with a lower return assumption for the first several years, then switching to a higher rate. The difference in outcomes illustrates exactly why sequence risk matters.

Common questions

Is there any way to fully eliminate sequence of returns risk?

Not entirely. Annuities convert a lump sum into guaranteed income, eliminating sequence risk for that portion of your portfolio — but they introduce other trade-offs (loss of flexibility, counterparty risk, inflation exposure). Most planners suggest managing rather than eliminating the risk through the strategies above, reserving annuitization for a portion of assets as a guaranteed income floor.

Does this risk go away as I get older in retirement?

It diminishes. As the portfolio gets smaller (from withdrawals) and the remaining retirement horizon shortens, there's less time for a bad sequence to do lasting damage — and less portfolio value at risk. The first decade is the most vulnerable; by your late 70s or 80s, the acute sequence risk has largely passed.

How does this relate to the 4% rule?

The 4% rule was derived from historical data that includes periods of bad sequence risk — the Great Depression, the 1970s stagflation, the early 2000s tech bust. The fact that 4% survived those periods in most historical scenarios is precisely because the research tested against actual sequences of returns, not just averages. But "survived in most historical scenarios" isn't a guarantee it survives every future scenario.

Should I keep more in bonds to protect against this?

A higher bond allocation in early retirement reduces sequence risk but also reduces expected long-term returns, which can shorten how long the portfolio lasts. The research is genuinely mixed on optimal asset allocation in early retirement. Many planners suggest enough bonds or cash to cover 2-3 years of expenses as a buffer, without going so conservative that growth potential is severely limited.
